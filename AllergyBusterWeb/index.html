The holidays are here

Before you gather, a few reminders.

Holiday meals mean new recipes, shared kitchens, food gifts and travel, and more chances for an allergen to slip through. Keep these in mind from Thanksgiving through New Year's.

🦃

Classic dishes hide allergens

Stuffing and gravy often contain wheat; mashed potatoes and casseroles, milk; eggnog, milk and eggs; pies and candied yams can include pecans or walnuts. Ask what went into each dish.

🍬

Seasonal versions can differ

Holiday-shaped candy and limited-edition treats can be made on different lines than the everyday version, with different "may contain" warnings. Check the label every time, even on a favorite.

🍪

Cookie trays & buffets

Shared platters and serving spoons spread allergens between dishes. Serve yourself first, use clean utensils, and skip homemade treats you can't confirm.

🥂

Check the drinks too

Some liqueurs are made with nuts or cream, and alcohol labels aren't required to list allergens. When in doubt, ask the host or the brand.

🎁

Food gifts & gift baskets

Homemade and repackaged gifts often have no full ingredient label. Ask the giver, or set it aside until you can check it.

🕯️

Scented gifts

Candles, lotions and gift sets often contain fragrance. If someone reacts to fragrance, check the ingredients before you wrap it.

✈️

Traveling? Pack the plan

Keep epinephrine in your carry-on, not checked bags, check expiration dates before you go, and pack safe snacks for delays.

📱

Scan it before you serve it

Scan holiday treats, gift sets and the ingredients for new recipes with AllergyBuster. Save the ones you trust so next year's shopping list is already done.

You can't always rely on others to keep you safe.

When you have a food allergy, every meal is a risk assessment. Ingredient labels are dense and confusing. Restaurant staff may not know what's in the kitchen. Friends and family mean well but don't always have the answers.

For millions of people, this isn't inconvenient — it's dangerous. A single hidden allergen can mean anaphylaxis, an ER visit, or worse. And it isn't only the major allergens: some additives, such as sulfites, can trigger reactions in sensitive people, which is why the FDA requires them on labels.
